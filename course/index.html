Career Clarity Workbook: From "I Want Out" to a Confident Next Step

A walkthrough of the Career Clarity Workbook. Six stages over about four weeks: take stock, know your raw material, generate options, filter, test, and decide, so you finish with three tested directions and one next step.

Get the PDF guide
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome to the Career Clarity Workbook. This is for people who know they want a different kind of work, but are stuck on the question, but what? The plan is to go from that feeling to three tested directions and one confident next step in about four weeks.

About. If this sounds like you, you are not alone. The guide mentions a large 2026 survey of UK career changers, and many of them feel stuck at exactly this point. Thinking harder rarely fixes it. A process does.

Start here. Most people already have plenty of information. What they lack is a process that turns vague feelings into a decision they trust. The workbook has six stages, and each one has worksheets that you fill in.

Test, then decide. Here is the big idea. Do not plan first and act later. Test first, then decide. You pick a few possible directions, try small pieces of them in real life, and let the evidence help you choose.

Maya. Along the way you meet Maya. She is a secondary English teacher with twelve years of experience. She is good at the job, but she is worn out by the workload. Her worked example shows you what a filled in worksheet looks like.

Stage 1: Take stock. Stage one is Take stock. Before you look forward, get precise about what is wrong now. First, separate the pushes from the pulls. Pushes drive you away from your job. Pulls draw you toward something new.

Keep, lose, gain. Next comes keep, lose and gain. What do you have now that you must not lose? What do you want to leave behind? And what do you want to gain? This makes vague unhappiness very specific.

Energy log. Then you keep a daily energy log. For each activity, rate how energised or drained you felt, and how absorbed you were. Look for the why behind the task, not just the task itself.

Stage 2: Raw material. Stage two is your raw material. Your next career is built from four things: what you value, what you are good at, what interests you, and what limits you have to respect.

Values. Start with values. Values are what make work feel worth doing. When a job clashes with your values, no salary fixes it for long. You choose your top five and write what each one means for you.

Strengths. Next are your strengths and transferable skills. Career changers often undersell themselves because they describe their skills in their old industry's language. Translate them into plain words that other fields understand.

Interests. Then interests. The guide uses a well known model by the psychologist John Holland, which groups interests into types. Take a free interest quiz and write down your top three types.

Non-negotiables. Limits are not the enemy of a career change. Ignoring them is. Write down your non negotiables early, like your real pay floor, so you do not fall for options you could never take.

Work profile. Then you pull everything onto a one page work profile. It becomes your filter for every option from now on. Keep it somewhere you will see it.

Stage 3: Options. Stage three is to generate options. Go wide on purpose. The aim is at least ten ideas from five different sources. Do not judge them yet.

Three futures. One exercise sketches three quite different five year plans. It works because it frees you from the idea that there is one right answer.

Stage 4: Filter. Stage four is the filter. Now you narrow down using your profile instead of your mood. You cut ten ideas to five, research each one, and then score them.

Scoring. A weighted scoring grid turns a fuzzy comparison into a clear one. It will not make the decision for you, but it shows why you prefer one option. Use ones and fives, and avoid scoring everything in the middle.

Stage 5: Test. Stage five is the one most people skip, and the one that creates real clarity. You talk to people doing the work, and try a small piece of it yourself, while you are still in your current job.

Conversations. A twenty to thirty minute conversation with someone doing the job teaches you more than hours of reading. The workbook gives you message templates, good questions to ask, and a log to record what you learn.

Experiments. Conversations tell you what a job is like. Experiments tell you what you are like doing it. Plan one small experiment for each direction, and run a career premortem, where you imagine it failed and ask why.

Stage 6: Decide. Stage six is decide and commit. You do not need to decide your whole future. You only need to decide your next move, and make it concrete enough to happen.

30-day plan. Turn your decision into a thirty day plan of small, dated actions. Each one should take less than two hours, and be clearly done or not done.

Troubleshooting. If you finish stage two and still have no idea, that is normal. And if you lose momentum, book your next session and restart with the smallest action, like sending one message.

Resources. At the back is a list of free tools by country, and a progress tracker so you can compare where you end up with where you started. Remember, this is general guidance. Begin with stage one, and give it one hour this week.

30-Day Career Test Drive: Try a New Career Before You Commit

A walkthrough of the 30-Day Career Test Drive. Four weeks of small, low-cost experiments (conversations, watching the work and a mini-project) that help you decide to go, adjust or drop a career idea.

Get the PDF guide
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome to the thirty day Career Test Drive. This video shows you how to try a new career for a month, before you spend money or quit your job on it.

About this kit. Most people choose a new career from the outside. They read job adverts, look at salary tables and hear other people's stories. Then they train for a job they have never actually tried. This kit helps you test the job first, using small, cheap experiments.

Start here. Here is the key idea. You are not trying to prove the career is right. You are trying to find out, as cheaply as possible, whether it is wrong. That small change in thinking saves a lot of time and money.

Week 1. Week one is hypothesis and conversations. You write down what you believe about the career, and then you go and talk to people who can tell you if you are right.

Week 1: hypothesis. A hypothesis is a set of beliefs that could turn out to be false. Write yours clearly. For example, what you think the day to day work is, what it pays, and why you think you would enjoy it. Writing it down makes it easy to check later.

Week 1: conversations. Then aim for three conversations. Ideally with someone new in the job, someone experienced, and someone who hires. Each one gives you a different view of the same career.

Week 2. Week two is about seeing the work. Conversations tell you what people say about a job. Watching tells you what it is actually like to be in the room.

Week 2: observing. There are several ways to observe. You can shadow someone for a few hours, sit in on a public event or class, or watch recorded talks from people doing the job. Pick at least one and write down what surprised you.

Week 3. Week three is the experiment that matters most. You do a small, real task from the job, and you notice whether you enjoy it.

Week 3: mini-project. Pick a task a junior person would do in their first month, and keep it small. Then plan it, do it, and review it. The review is important, because it shows how you felt while doing it, not just whether you finished.

Week 4. Week four is the verdict. You look at all the evidence against your original hypothesis, and you make a decision you can explain.

Fit scorecard. The fit scorecard helps you decide. You score each area from one to five, based on what you saw and did, not what you hoped. If you reach twenty four or more out of thirty, that is a go. Between fifteen and twenty three, adjust. Under fifteen, drop it and be glad you found out cheaply.

Scripts. At the back there are message scripts, for example for asking for an introduction. They save you from staring at a blank screen. Keep your messages short, polite and specific.

Troubleshooting. If nobody replies, do not give up. Send more requests than you need. About three asks for every conversation is normal. Then follow up kindly once. That is the whole kit. Start with week one today.

Career Change Money Planner: Know Your Runway Before You Quit

A walkthrough of the Career Change Money Planner. Learn your real monthly spending, your runway in months, the pay cut you can survive, how big your bridge fund should be, and your money-ready date.

Get the PDF guide
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome to the Career Change Money Planner. This guide helps you work out how long you can afford a career change, how big your bridge fund should be, and what pay cut you could survive, before you hand in your notice.

About. Money is the reason many career changes stay a daydream, and the reason some go badly wrong. Some people never work out the cost, so the fear stays vague. Others leap without a number and run out of savings. This planner replaces the fear with real numbers.

Start here. You do not need to be rich to change careers. You need to know your numbers and build in enough margin to make good decisions instead of desperate ones. First, gather three months of bank and card statements and your latest payslip.

Step 1. Step one is your real monthly number. Most people underestimate their spending, so use your statements, not your memory.

Essential or flexible. Sort each cost into essential or flexible. Essential is what you would still pay in a lean month, like housing, bills, basic food, transport, minimum debt payments and insurance. Flexible is what you could pause.

Worked example. There is a worked example, Alex's monthly spending, so you can see how a filled in page looks before you do your own.

Step 2: Runway. Step two is your runway. Runway is how many months your savings would cover the gap between what you need and what you earn during the change.

The formula. The formula is simple. Your monthly need is your essential spending plus a ten percent safety buffer. Then subtract the income you can count on, like part time work or a partner's contribution. What is left is your monthly gap. Divide your savings by that gap, and you get your runway in months.

Step 3: Pay cut. Step three is the pay cut you can survive. Many career changes start with a lower salary. If you know your floor in advance, you can judge offers calmly.

Your floor. Your floor is the take home pay that covers essential spending plus the minimum you want for saving and life. Above the floor is comfortable. Below it means running down savings every month.

Step 4: Bridge fund. Step four is the bridge fund. It pays for the months between leaving your old income and reaching your new one. Estimate how long the change will take. If you are unsure, use six months. Then add one off costs like course fees and travel to interviews.

Progress tracker. Then set a monthly saving amount and use the progress tracker to see your fund grow. Seeing the percentage rise is surprisingly motivating.

Step 5: Date. Step five is your money ready date. You combine your numbers into one decision: when you can move, and what would make you pause.

Transition models. Choose a transition model. Stay and build is the slowest and safest, where you keep your job and retrain in the evenings. Reduce hours means going part time while you build skills. A bridge job is a less demanding job that frees time and energy. And the full leap is the fastest, but the highest risk. Each one needs a different amount of money.

Stop signals. Decide your stop signals now. For example, if my savings fall below a number, or if I have no interviews after a number of months, then I will pause or take temporary work. Deciding now protects you from panic later.

Troubleshooting. If your runway is under three months, choose stay and build for now, and use the time to save. A slower change that finishes is better than a fast one that collapses. If you have debts, count the minimum payments as essential spending. This is general guidance, not financial advice. Start with step one today.

Retraining Route Decision Kit: Choose the Right Course Without Wasting Money

A walkthrough of the Retraining Route Decision Kit. Find out if you need to retrain at all, compare courses, degrees, bootcamps and apprenticeships, find funding, vet providers and calculate true cost.

Get the PDF guide
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome to the Retraining Route Decision Kit. This guide helps you find out whether you need to retrain at all, and if you do, how to choose a route on true cost and time instead of on advertising.

About. This kit is for people who already have a career direction in mind, maybe from the Career Clarity Workbook, and now face the expensive question: do I need to retrain, and how? Courses, degrees and bootcamps all promise a lot, so you need a way to compare them fairly.

Start here. The most expensive mistake in a career change is paying for the wrong training. The second most expensive is not getting training you actually need. This kit helps you avoid both.

Starting point. First, record your starting point. Write down your target role, the country where you want to work, and the time and money you can spend.

Step 1. Step one: do you need to retrain? Before comparing courses, find out what employers in your target role actually require. Many people find they need far less than they feared.

Regulated or not. Some jobs are regulated. You legally cannot do them without a specific qualification or registration. Others are not. Check which one yours is, because it changes everything.

Ten-advert audit. Then do the ten advert audit. Collect ten real job adverts, and count how many ask for each degree, skill or tool. Confirm what you find by talking to one or two people doing the job.

Skills vs credentials. Remember, skills are not the same as credentials. A job may need a skill without needing a certificate to prove it. In that case, the cheapest route might be learning the skill and showing a project.

Step 2. Step two is to map your routes. There is almost always more than one way into a career. List every realistic route first, so you compare options instead of just reacting to the first course advert you see.

Route types. The table covers self study with a portfolio, short courses, bootcamps, apprenticeships, vocational qualifications, part time and full time degrees, and internal moves. For each one it says when it is best, what to watch out for, and how long it usually takes.

Step 3: Funding. Step three is to find the funding. Many adults pay full price for training they could have had free or cheaper. Check what exists in your country before you pay anything.

Funding by country. The guide lists examples of schemes to look for in different countries. Rules and budgets change, so always check the official site for the current terms.

Step 4: Vet. Step four is to vet the provider. The same type of course can be excellent at one provider and worthless at another. Judge providers on independent evidence, not on marketing.

Independent data. The guide shows where to find independent data, and the provider check gives you questions to ask, like how many students finish, and how many get jobs.

Step 5: True cost. Step five is the true cost and the decision. Course fees are often the smallest part of what retraining costs. You add fees, materials, travel, and the income you lose while studying.

Jo's example. In Jo's worked example, a master's degree looked the most serious. But a year of lost income made it the most expensive, and it would take about five years of higher pay to earn back. Her audit showed employers did not require it. A free bootcamp plus a portfolio met every requirement she found.

Choose. Now compare routes on the same basis. Score each one from one to five, weight what matters most to you, and choose. Then write down your decision, your budget and how you will pay for it.

Troubleshooting. If every advert asks for a degree but people say it does not matter, check whether it is listed as essential or desirable. Ask people in the job. This is general guidance. Start with the starting point page today.

CV & LinkedIn Translator: Speak the Language Your New Field Hires For

A walkthrough of the CV & LinkedIn Translator. Build a keyword bank from job adverts, turn your old duties into results, rewrite your CV and LinkedIn profile, and prepare a clear answer to why you are changing careers.

Get the PDF guide
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome to the CV and LinkedIn Translator. It helps you turn experience from your old career into the language your new field hires for. You will end up with a rewritten CV, a LinkedIn profile that tells one story, and a short answer to why the change.

About. You have years of real experience. The problem is that it is written in the language of your old job. A recruiter in your new field reads your CV for only a few seconds, does not recognise the words, and moves on.

Start here. Employers are not refusing your experience. Usually they simply cannot see it, because it is described in the wrong words. The kit shows the common problems and the fix for each.

Translation brief. Start with your translation brief. Write your target job title, the type of employer, your current job title, and the years of experience you can draw on.

Step 1. Step one is to learn the target language. Job adverts are the best free dictionary you will ever get.

Keyword bank. Collect eight to ten current adverts for your target role. Highlight three kinds of words: skills, tools and outcomes. Then count how often each one appears. The words that appear most go into your keyword bank.

Jargon swap. Next is the jargon swap list. List words from your old field that an outsider would not understand, and find the plain equivalent. Do it before you rewrite, so you catch jargon you no longer notice.

Step 2. Step two is to mine your experience. Most people undersell themselves because they only remember their job description. This step digs out specific situations where you did the things your new field values.

Evidence formula. Every strong bullet has the same three parts. An action verb, what you did in target field words, and the result, ideally with a number. Time, money, volume, people or percentage.

Before and after. Here is a before and after. Before: responsible for staff rotas. After: scheduled a team across seven day operations and cut overtime hours. Same person, same job, but now the value is clear. Aim for at least twelve stories like this.

Step 3. Step three is to rewrite the CV. Put your most relevant evidence where a skimming reader will see it first.

CV layout. The career change layout starts with a headline that names your target role, not your old title. Then a short profile, then key skills, then your evidence, and last your history.

Cover note. There is also a cover note template. Say why this role at this organisation, what you have done that matches their top two requirements, your one sentence reason for the change, and a clear next step.

Step 4: LinkedIn. Step four is to rebuild LinkedIn. Recruiters search by keyword and job title. If your profile still describes your old career, you will not appear in searches for the new one.

LinkedIn sections. Work through the headline, the About section, your experience entries, your skills and your featured section. Make the first two lines of your About section count, because that is all people see before they click see more.

Step 5: Story. Step five is to tell the story. Every interviewer will ask why you are changing careers. A clear, forward looking answer turns your change from a risk into a reason to hire you.

Bridge story. Use the bridge story formula. The past: what you did and the skill it built. The pivot: the moment you realised the target role's core work is what you love. The proof: what you have done since. And the future: where you are heading.

Consistency. Finally, run the consistency check. Your CV, LinkedIn and spoken story should name the same target role, the same top three strengths, and the same best piece of evidence.

Troubleshooting. If you still get no replies, check three things. Are you applying to roles where you meet most of the essentials? Does the top of page one show your target role and best evidence? And are you applying only cold? Warm introductions help. Start with your translation brief today.

Foot in the Door Playbook: Get Your First Role in a New Field

A walkthrough of the Foot in the Door Playbook. Five plays for career changers with no direct experience: a proof project, warm introductions, stepping-stone roles, inside moves and targeted applications.

Get the PDF guide
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome to the Foot in the Door Playbook. It is for career changers who need to land their first role in a new field, without the usual years of experience.

About. The hardest job in a career change is the first one. Adverts ask for two years of experience in a similar role, and you cannot get the experience without the job. This playbook gives you five practical plays to get around that.

Start here. Here is the core idea. No experience usually means no evidence I can see. Every play in this book either creates evidence, or puts it in front of someone who can say yes. Employers increasingly look beyond job titles to skills.

Play 1. Play one is the proof project. A proof project is a small, real piece of the target work, done well and written up, that you can point to in applications and interviews.

Good proof projects. A good one is real, relevant, and small. Real means it solves a genuine problem for a real person, like a charity, a small business or a club. Relevant means it uses at least three of the top skills from job adverts. Small means you can finish it in about fifteen to twenty five hours.

Case study. Then write a one page case study. The problem, what you did, the result, and what you would do next time. That page becomes your evidence.

Play 2. Play two is warm introductions. An application that arrives with a recommendation from someone the hiring manager trusts gets read differently from a cold one.

Contact map. Map your contacts in three circles. You need far fewer people than you think, and most introductions come from the second circle, the friends of people you know.

Thank-you note. After every chat, send a short thank you. Mention one specific thing you did with their advice, ask if they know anyone else to speak to, and attach your case study.

Play 3. Play three is stepping stone roles. If your target role is out of reach today, find the role one step away that will hire you now and gets you inside the field.

Examples. The table shows examples. For instance, if you want IT support, a service desk role or an apprenticeship can be the first step. Pick three job titles to search for.

Play 4. Play four is inside moves. Your current employer already trusts you. If it has your target role anywhere in the organisation, an internal move can skip the experience barrier.

Inside steps. Find out which teams do the work you want. Offer to help them on a small project. Then talk to your manager about a move.

Play 5. Play five is targeted applications. Twenty generic applications usually achieve less than five targeted ones. Apply where you have an edge, and show it.

Your edge. Your edge might be employers in your old industry who need the new skill, small growing organisations where people wear many hats, or roles where your old field is useful.

Scoreboard. Track activity you control, not outcomes you do not. A steady weekly rhythm might be two conversations, two hours on proof work, and three targeted applications.

Fortnightly review. Every two weeks, ask which play is producing replies, and do more of it.

Troubleshooting. If you apply and hear nothing, shift effort to plays two and three. If you get interviews but no offers, ask for feedback every time and practise telling your proof project as a story. Start with play one today.

90-Day Career Change Planner: Thirteen Weeks of Small Actions

A walkthrough of the 90-Day Career Change Planner. Set a 90-day goal, plan three small actions a week for thirteen weeks, use if-then plans, and review each month so you keep moving.

Get the PDF guide
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome to the ninety day Career Change Planner. It turns a decided career change into thirteen weeks of small, scheduled actions, with weekly reviews and if then plans that keep you moving when life gets busy.

About. You have decided on a direction. Now the challenge changes. It is no longer what should I do, but how do I keep making progress when I am tired and busy and nobody is checking? Most career changes stall here, because the plan is too big and too vague.

Start here. The planner is built around one idea. Small actions, scheduled for a specific day and time, beat big intentions. You set your goal once, then plan and review a little each week.

If-then plans. The planner uses if then plans. For example, if I feel like giving up after a rejection, then I will message my accountability partner before doing anything else. A research review found that plans like this help people reach goals.

Set up. Set up takes twenty minutes. You will finish with one goal, three milestones and a fixed weekly planning slot.

90-day goal. Write your ninety day goal. Your target direction, your start and end dates, and what you will have by the end that is specific and within your control.

Weekly rhythm. Then set your weekly rhythm. Pick a regular planning slot, your work sessions, and an accountability partner. Telling one person your plan makes it much more likely to happen.

Weeks 1 to 4. Weeks one to four are the foundation phase. Each week has three actions. Week one, book your planning slot and tell one person. Week two, collect job adverts and list the skills that keep appearing. Week three, book your first conversation.

Week 4. In week four, you review the month. What is working? What should you change for the next phase?

Month 1 review. The month one review counts how many of the twelve actions you did, whether you reached the milestone, and what was the most useful thing you did.

Weeks 5 to 9. Weeks five to nine are the build phase. You turn research into evidence: skills, a proof project and a profile that points at your new direction.

Build weeks. Start your proof project, add a new contact, update your CV headline and LinkedIn, and share early work for feedback. Week nine is another review.

Month 2 review. The month two review asks what evidence you now have that you did not have before. It is a good way to see progress.

Weeks 10 to 13. Weeks ten to thirteen are the launch phase. You put your evidence in front of people who can say yes, and keep following up.

Launch weeks. Finish your proof project, send your first targeted applications, practise your why the change story out loud, and follow up every lead.

Week 13. In week thirteen, you do a final review and plan your next ninety days.

90-day review. The ninety day review counts your thirty nine actions, and asks whether you reached your goal fully, partly or not yet. Any answer is useful. Print the planner and start with the set up page today.

12-Week Roadmap and Win Log: The Order to Use Your Career Change Guides

A walkthrough of the 12-Week Roadmap and Win Log. See which guide to use in which week, what task to finish each week, and how to keep a win log so you can see your progress.

Part of the complete program.
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome. This video walks you through the 12-Week Roadmap and Win Log. It is the guide that tells you the order to use your other career change guides in. If you have ever bought several guides and wondered where to start, this is the answer.

What it is. The roadmap spreads your nine guides over twelve weeks. So every week you know exactly what to work on, and you never have to guess what comes next. It is general guidance, and you can move at your own speed.

How to use it. The rule is simple. One topic, one task and one review each week. That is all. Do not try to do three guides at once. Pick the week you are in, do the task, and then do a short review at the end.

The 12-week map. This is the twelve week map. Weeks one to three use the Career Clarity Workbook, so you find out what you want. Weeks four and five use the thirty day Career Test Drive, so you try the work before you commit.

The 12-week map. Week six is the Money Planner, where you find your runway. Week seven is the Retraining Route Decision Kit. Weeks eight and nine are the CV and LinkedIn Translator. Weeks ten and eleven are the Foot in the Door Playbook. And week twelve sets up your next ninety days.

Weeks 1 to 3. In weeks one to three, you work through the Career Clarity Workbook. First your values, strengths and interests. Then you generate real options and score them. Then you choose your top three and plan small experiments.

Weeks 4 and 5. In week four, you write a hypothesis and start your experiments from the Test Drive. In week five, you watch the work, work alongside someone, and reach a verdict. The goal is to find out cheaply whether the career is wrong for you.

Weeks 6 and 7. Week six is about money. You split your spending, find your runway, and work out your lowest acceptable offer. Week seven is about training. You run the ten advert test, check funding options, and work out the true cost.

Weeks 8 and 9. Weeks eight and nine are about how you present yourself. You build your keyword bank, rewrite your duties as results, and then line up your CV, your LinkedIn profile and your why-the-change story, so they all tell one story.

Weeks 10 to 12. In weeks ten and eleven, you create proof, ask for introductions, look at stepping stone roles, and send targeted applications. Week twelve is where you set up your next ninety days and run your first planning week.

The win log. Now the win log. Write down anything that moved you forward. A conversation, a finished task, a reply, a decision. Small wins count. Read the log back when you doubt yourself, because it is proof that you are moving.

12-week review. At the end there is a twelve week review, so you can see where you are now compared with where you started. Take your time with it, and then choose your next step. Good luck, and take it one week at a time.

Teacher Exit Kit: Leave the Classroom With a Plan

A walkthrough of the Teacher Exit Kit. Decide whether to leave, adjust or stay, map where teachers go, translate teaching skills for employers, test and apply, and time your exit around the school year.

Get the PDF guide
Read the script

Welcome. Welcome to the Teacher Exit Kit. It is for teachers ready to leave the classroom. It helps you map where teaching skills transfer, translate them for employers, and plan an exit that fits the school year.

About. You are not alone in thinking about leaving. In England, about thirty eight thousand six hundred full time equivalent qualified teachers left state funded schools in 2024 to 2025. That is roughly one in twelve.

Start here. Teachers do not lack skills. They lack a translation, a map of options outside the school gate, and a plan that respects the school calendar. Along the way you meet Hannah, a worked example.

Step 1. Step one: leave, adjust, or stay? Before mapping new careers, be clear about what exactly you are leaving. Sometimes the problem is teaching. Sometimes it is this school, this phase, or this workload.

Keep, lose, unsure. Sort the parts of your job into keep, lose and unsure. Be specific. Say explaining a new idea and seeing it click, not just teaching.

My verdict. Then write your verdict. What you are really leaving, in one sentence, and three things your next role must have.

Step 2. Step two is to map where teachers go. Teaching skills lead to more places than most teachers realise. The guide maps options in three rings.

Three rings. Ring one is close to school, ring two is education adjacent, and ring three is further afield. For each, the table lists example roles and the teaching skills they use.

Role scorer. Use the role shortlist scorer to score each role against your must haves and practical factors. Aim for a shortlist of two.
